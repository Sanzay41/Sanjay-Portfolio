Where can Dharan grow safely?

Risk-sensitive land use planning for Dharan Sub-Metropolitan City: how land cover is changing, where land is suitable, and where urban nodes belong.

  1. ProblemWhere can Dharan grow safely?
  2. DataLand cover 2004–2025, DEM, slope, roads, rivers, forest, settlements
  3. MethodologyCA–Markov simulation, AHP weighted overlay, node analysis
  4. GIS processingSix layers reclassified 1–5, then forest, water and hazard masks
  5. Results2050 BAU scenario; about 2,840 ha developable after masking
  6. Planning implicationsDirect growth to classes 4–5; keep hazard zones excluded
Location
Dharan Sub-Metropolitan City, eastern Nepal
Context
Risk-sensitive land use planning
My role
GIS analysis: land-cover change simulation, suitability modelling and node identification
Tools
ArcGIS Pro, TerrSet (Cellular Automata–Markov)
Data
Multi-date land-cover maps from historical imagery; DEM, slope, river, road, settlement and forest layers
Outputs
Land-cover maps 2004–2050, suitability and masked-suitability maps, node hierarchy and area allocation
The planning problem. Dharan sits between steep forested hills to the north and a large protected forest to the south. Growth has to fit in a narrow band of buildable land, away from rivers and unstable slopes. Planners need to know how fast built-up land is likely to expand, which land can take it, and where to concentrate it.

1 · Trend

Land-cover change, 2004 to 2050

Select a year. Class areas update with each map and come directly from the model tables.

    Scenario output for planning support, not a forecast. Class areas for the simulated 2025 map agree with the observed 2025 map to within 1.8% for every class.

    Method

    Land cover was mapped for 2004, 2016 and 2025 using four classes: agriculture, built-up, forest and water. A Cellular Automata–Markov model in TerrSet used the observed change to simulate 2025 and then project 2034, 2042 and 2050. The simulated 2025 map was compared with the observed 2025 map as a validation checkpoint: the class areas differed by 0.29% (agriculture), 0.42% (built-up), under 0.01% (forest) and 1.78% (water).

    What it shows

    Under business as usual, built-up land roughly doubles from 1,097 ha in 2004 to 2,296 ha in 2050, while agriculture falls by about a quarter. Forest stays near 70% of the area.

    2 · Capability

    Which land can take the growth?

    A GIS multi-criteria analysis with AHP-derived weights. Every layer is reclassified to the same 1–5 scale.

    Weights and model

    The suitability index is the weighted sum of the six reclassified rasters: 0.15 forest + 0.20 river + 0.15 settlement + 0.20 road + 0.20 slope + 0.10 elevation. The pairwise matrix was built from these weights and has a consistency ratio of 0.00%, below the 10% threshold.

    River, road and slope together carry 60% of the weight. This surface is a first, preliminary ranking made before the legal and safety constraints are applied.

    3 · Constraint

    Constraints come last, and they are not negotiable

    Forest and water are removed first. Flood and landslide zones are removed on top. A high score cannot offset a safety risk.

    The natural-resource mask removes 1,242 ha (28.5%) of the best land and the hazard mask a further 274 ha, a net loss of 1,516 ha (34.8%). Very high suitability barely changes because it already sits on safe, built-up ground. Flood zones cover 1,222 ha and landslide zones 1,945 ha.

    An evidence-based hierarchy of urban nodes

    Planning implications

    What the analysis recommends

    • Direct development to high-suitability land. About 2,840 ha remains developable after both masks.
    • Enforce constraints as hard exclusions for forest, water, flood and landslide zones, whatever the score.
    • Verify on site. Use the surface as a macro-level decision tool, supported by detailed geotechnical field validation.
