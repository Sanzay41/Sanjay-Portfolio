Dharan Risk-Sensitive Land Use Planning

Land-cover change simulation, multi-criteria land suitability and urban node identification for Dharan Sub-Metropolitan City.

Location
Dharan Sub-Metropolitan City, Sunsari District, Koshi Province
Assignment
Risk-sensitive land use planning
Organisation
Picasso Engineering Consultancy
My role
GIS analyst: land-cover mapping, change simulation, suitability modelling, constraint masking and urban node identification
Software
ArcGIS Pro; TerrSet (Cellular Automata–Markov)
Data
Land-cover maps for 2004, 2016 and 2025 classified from historical Google Earth imagery; DEM and slope; river, road, settlement and forest layers; flood and landslide hazard zones
Study area
About 19,200 ha (see the note on area totals below)
Outputs
Land-cover maps 2004–2050, preliminary and masked suitability maps, and an urban node hierarchy with area allocation

Background and problem

Dharan lies between steep forested hills to the north and a large protected forest to the south. New development has to fit within a narrow band of buildable land, away from rivers and unstable slopes. The planning team needed to know how fast built-up land is likely to expand, which land can accommodate it safely, and where growth should be concentrated.

Objectives

  1. Quantify land-cover change from 2004 to 2025 and project it to 2050 under a business-as-usual scenario.
  2. Rank land suitability for urban development using six AHP-weighted criteria.
  3. Exclude natural-resource and hazard areas as hard constraints.
  4. Identify primary, secondary and tertiary urban nodes to guide where growth concentrates.

Workflow

  1. ProblemWhere can Dharan grow safely?
  2. DataLand cover 2004–2025, DEM, roads, rivers, forest, settlements
  3. MethodCA–Markov simulation, AHP weighted overlay, node analysis
  4. ProcessingSix layers reclassified 1–5, then forest, water and hazard masks
  5. Results2050 scenario; about 2,840 ha of high or very high suitability after masking
  6. ImplicationsDirect growth to suitable land; keep hazard zones excluded
Step 1

Land-cover change, 2004 to 2050

Select a year. Class areas update with each map and come directly from the model output tables.

    Observed maps (2004, 2016, 2025) and modelled maps (simulated 2025 and the 2034–2050 scenario) are labelled separately. Scenario maps support planning; they are not forecasts.

    Method

    Land cover was mapped for 2004, 2016 and 2025 in four classes: agriculture, built-up, forest and water. A Cellular Automata–Markov model in TerrSet used the observed transitions to simulate 2025 and then project 2034, 2042 and 2050.

    Model check

    The simulated 2025 map was compared with the observed 2025 map by class area. The differences were 0.29% for agriculture, 0.42% for built-up land, under 0.01% for forest and 1.78% for water.

    What the check does and does not show

    Close class totals show that the model reproduces how much of each class exists. They do not show that change is placed in the right locations, and they say nothing about the accuracy of the classified input maps. Cell-by-cell agreement statistics and a classification accuracy assessment have not been reported for this study.

    Results

    Under business as usual, built-up land roughly doubles, from 1,097 ha in 2004 to 2,296 ha in 2050, while agricultural land falls by about a quarter, from 3,500 ha to 2,642 ha. Forest remains close to 70% of the area throughout.

    Step 2

    Which land can accommodate growth?

    A GIS multi-criteria analysis with AHP-derived weights. Each criterion is reclassified to the same 1–5 scale before the weighted overlay.

    Weights and model

    The suitability index is the weighted sum of the six reclassified rasters: 0.20 river + 0.20 road + 0.20 slope + 0.15 forest + 0.15 settlement + 0.10 elevation. River, road and slope together carry 60% of the weight.

    The pairwise comparison matrix was derived directly from these weights, so it is perfectly consistent (consistency ratio 0.00, below the 0.10 threshold). The weights therefore express expert judgement rather than the result of an independent pairwise elicitation.

    This surface is a preliminary ranking, produced before legal and safety constraints are applied. The AHP method in detail

    DharanResolvedProject evidence

    Hazardous land scoring as highly suitable

    A cell on a flood-prone riverbank could still score “High” because a weighted overlay lets strong criteria offset a weak one. Hard masks after the overlay removed 1,516 ha of apparently suitable land.

    Problem, cause, investigation, solution, verification and outcome
    Problem
    In the six-criteria weighted overlay, land within 50 m of a river scores 1 on the river criterion but can still reach a high suitability index.
    Root cause
    Weighted linear combination is compensatory: a low score on one criterion is averaged against high scores on the others. A safety limit cannot be expressed as a weight.
    Investigation
    Worked example for a riverbank cell that scores 5 on every other layer: SI = 0.15(5) + 0.20(1) + 0.15(5) + 0.20(5) + 0.20(5) + 0.10(5) = 4.20, which falls in the High class.
    Solution
    Kept the overlay for ranking relative suitability, then applied two non-compensatory masks after classification: first forest and water bodies (14,424 ha), then flood (1,222.3 ha) and landslide (1,945.4 ha) zones.
    Verification
    Tabulated High and Very High land after each stage: 4,355.6 ha after the overlay, 3,113.3 ha after the natural-resource mask and 2,839.7 ha after the hazard mask.
    Outcome
    The figure given to planners, about 2,840 ha, excludes forest, water, flood and landslide land by construction. Very High land was unchanged because it already lay on safe, built-up ground.

    Lesson: Express legal and safety limits as masks, not as low scores.

    Area totals that do not reconcile

    The suitability classes total 19,023.3 ha but the land-cover maps total 19,213.8 ha. Stage-by-stage totals point to empty (NoData) cells in the overlay rather than a different boundary.

    Problem, cause, investigation, solution, verification and outcome
    Problem
    The preliminary suitability classes cover 19,023.3 ha, about 190 ha (1%) less than the land-cover maps.
    Root cause
    Most likely NoData cells in one or more criterion rasters, for example at the edge of the DEM or a distance surface. A weighted overlay returns NoData wherever any input is empty.
    Investigation
    Class totals rise at every masking stage: 19,023.3 ha after the overlay, 19,165.4 ha after the natural-resource mask and 19,209.6 ha after the hazard mask, approaching the land-cover total. That pattern fits mask layers assigning their classes to cells the overlay left empty; a different study boundary would not produce it.
    Solution
    Planned: locate the empty cells within the study-area mask, trace which criterion causes them, extend or fill that layer, and rerun every stage on one snap raster, cell size and processing extent.
    Verification
    Planned: every stage should total the same study area.
    Outcome
    Both figures are reported as produced, with the likely cause documented, rather than silently adjusted.

    Lesson: Check class totals against the study-area area after every step, not only at the end.

    Showing that the land-change model is credible

    The simulated 2025 map reproduces observed class areas to within 1.8%. That confirms the amount of change, not its location, so cell-level agreement is the next test.

    Problem, cause, investigation, solution, verification and outcome
    Problem
    A business-as-usual projection to 2050 needs evidence that the model behaves realistically.
    Root cause
    A CA–Markov model can reproduce how much land changes class while placing that change in the wrong cells.
    Investigation
    Simulated 2025 from the earlier maps and compared it with the observed 2025 map. Class-area differences: agriculture 0.29%, built-up 0.42%, forest under 0.01%, water 1.78%.
    Solution
    Reported the result as quantity agreement only, and labelled observed, simulated and projected maps separately on every output.
    Verification
    Planned: cell-by-cell comparison of simulated and observed 2025 (Kappa variants and figure of merit), and an accuracy assessment of the classified input maps.
    Outcome
    The projected quantities are credible; the accuracy of the spatial allocation is not yet measured and is stated as such.

    Lesson: Class-area agreement, allocation accuracy and classification accuracy are three different checks.

    DharanResolvedConfirmed by Sanjay

    Preparing historical land-cover rasters for TerrSet

    The land-cover rasters and the DEM did not share one grid, and class codes did not match their categories consistently, especially built-up (Class 3). They were harmonised before change modelling.

    Problem, cause, investigation, solution, verification and outcome
    Problem
    The historical land-cover rasters and the DEM differed in coordinate system, cell size, extent and grid alignment, and the raster class codes did not consistently match their land-cover categories, particularly the built-up class (Class 3).
    Root cause
    The layers came from different sources and classification sessions. TerrSet compares maps cell by cell, so it needs identical grids and one class legend across all dates.
    Investigation
    Compared the raster properties and class legends of each date against the DEM.
    Solution
    Brought every raster onto one reference grid and recoded the classes to a single four-class legend (agriculture, built-up, forest, water) before running change analysis.
    Verification
    Every map in the series now covers the same area: 19,213.8 ha in 2004, 19,214.0 ha in 2016 and 19,213.0 ha in 2025. The simulated 2025 map also reproduces observed class totals.
    Outcome
    A consistent map series that the change model can compare date by date.

    Lesson: Fix the grid and the legend before modelling; errors here propagate into every transition.

    DharanMethod noteProject evidence

    A consistency ratio of exactly zero

    The AHP matrix was built from preset weights (aᵢⱼ = wᵢ/wⱼ), so it is perfectly consistent by construction. The weights are therefore expert-assigned, not derived from pairwise judgements.

    Problem, cause, investigation, solution, verification and outcome
    Problem
    The suitability model reports CR = 0.00%, which can read as strong validation.
    Root cause
    Each matrix entry equals the ratio of two preset weights. Such a matrix always has λmax = n, so CI and CR are zero whatever the weights are.
    Investigation
    Recomputed the matrix: λmax = 6.000, CI = 0, CR = 0, and the eigenvector returns exactly the input weights (20, 20, 20, 15, 15 and 10%).
    Solution
    The case study now describes the weights as expert-assigned. The next revision should elicit pairwise judgements on Saaty’s 1–9 scale, derive weights and CR from them, and test sensitivity.
    Outcome
    The method is described accurately, and the consistency ratio is not presented as evidence of map accuracy.

    Lesson: CR checks the logic of judgements; it says nothing about whether the map is right.

    Planning implications

    • Direct development to high-suitability land. About 2,840 ha of high and very high suitability remains after both masks.
    • Treat constraints as hard exclusions for forest, water, flood and landslide zones, regardless of the suitability score.
    • Concentrate growth in the node hierarchy rather than allowing it to spread along every road.
    • Verify on site. The surface is a macro-level decision tool and needs detailed geotechnical field validation before land is allocated.

    My contribution

    • Classified land cover for 2004, 2016 and 2025 from historical Google Earth imagery.
    • Built and ran the Cellular Automata–Markov model in TerrSet and compared the simulated and observed 2025 maps.
    • Prepared, reclassified and weighted the six suitability criteria and produced the weighted overlay in ArcGIS Pro.
    • Applied the natural-resource and hazard masks and calculated the area tables.
    • Identified and mapped the urban node hierarchy and produced the map sheets.

    Limitations and next steps

    • Validation is area-based. The next step is cell-level agreement against the observed 2025 map (for example Kappa variants or figure of merit).
    • Classification accuracy of the input land-cover maps should be documented with a confusion matrix from reference points.
    • Area totals differ. The land-cover maps total 19,213.8 ha and the suitability raster 19,023.3 ha. The likely cause is NoData cells in the overlay (see the investigation); it has not yet been corrected.
    • Weights. The six weights are expert-assigned (CR = 0 by construction). Varying them would show how stable the high-suitability areas are.
    • Scale. Results are macro-level and do not replace site investigation.
