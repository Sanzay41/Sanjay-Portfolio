Dharan Risk-Sensitive Land Use Planning

Land-cover change simulation, multi-criteria land suitability and urban node identification for Dharan Sub-Metropolitan City.

Location
Dharan Sub-Metropolitan City, Sunsari District, Koshi Province
Assignment
Risk-sensitive land use planning
Organisation
Picasso Engineering Consultancy
My role
GIS analyst: land-cover mapping, change simulation, suitability modelling, constraint masking and urban node identification
Software
ArcGIS Pro; TerrSet (Cellular Automata–Markov)
Data
Land-cover maps for 2004, 2016 and 2025 classified from historical Google Earth imagery; DEM and slope; river, road, settlement and forest layers; flood and landslide hazard zones
Study area
About 19,200 ha (see the note on area totals below)
Outputs
Land-cover maps 2004–2050, preliminary and masked suitability maps, and an urban node hierarchy with area allocation

Background and problem

Dharan lies between steep forested hills to the north and a large protected forest to the south. New development has to fit within a narrow band of buildable land, away from rivers and unstable slopes. The planning team needed to know how fast built-up land is likely to expand, which land can accommodate it safely, and where growth should be concentrated.

Objectives

  1. Quantify land-cover change from 2004 to 2025 and project it to 2050 under a business-as-usual scenario.
  2. Rank land suitability for urban development using six AHP-weighted criteria.
  3. Exclude natural-resource and hazard areas as hard constraints.
  4. Identify primary, secondary and tertiary urban nodes to guide where growth concentrates.

Workflow

  1. ProblemWhere can Dharan grow safely?
  2. DataLand cover 2004–2025, DEM, roads, rivers, forest, settlements
  3. MethodCA–Markov simulation, AHP weighted overlay, node analysis
  4. ProcessingSix layers reclassified 1–5, then forest, water and hazard masks
  5. Results2050 scenario; about 2,840 ha of high or very high suitability after masking
  6. ImplicationsDirect growth to suitable land; keep hazard zones excluded
Step 1

Land-cover change, 2004 to 2050

Select a year. Class areas update with each map and come directly from the model output tables.

    Observed maps (2004, 2016, 2025) and modelled maps (simulated 2025 and the 2034–2050 scenario) are labelled separately. Scenario maps support planning; they are not forecasts.

    Method

    Land cover was mapped for 2004, 2016 and 2025 in four classes: agriculture, built-up, forest and water. A Cellular Automata–Markov model in TerrSet used the observed transitions to simulate 2025 and then project 2034, 2042 and 2050.

    Model check

    The simulated 2025 map was compared with the observed 2025 map by class area. The differences were 0.29% for agriculture, 0.42% for built-up land, under 0.01% for forest and 1.78% for water.

    What the check does and does not show

    Close class totals show that the model reproduces how much of each class exists. They do not show that change is placed in the right locations, and they say nothing about the accuracy of the classified input maps. Cell-by-cell agreement statistics and a classification accuracy assessment have not been reported for this study.

    Results

    Under business as usual, built-up land roughly doubles, from 1,097 ha in 2004 to 2,296 ha in 2050, while agricultural land falls by about a quarter, from 3,500 ha to 2,642 ha. Forest remains close to 70% of the area throughout.

    Step 2

    Which land can accommodate growth?

    A GIS multi-criteria analysis with AHP-derived weights. Each criterion is reclassified to the same 1–5 scale before the weighted overlay.

    Weights and model

    The suitability index is the weighted sum of the six reclassified rasters: 0.20 river + 0.20 road + 0.20 slope + 0.15 forest + 0.15 settlement + 0.10 elevation. River, road and slope together carry 60% of the weight.

    The pairwise comparison matrix was derived directly from these weights, so it is perfectly consistent (consistency ratio 0.00, below the 0.10 threshold). The weights therefore express expert judgement rather than the result of an independent pairwise elicitation.

    This surface is a preliminary ranking, produced before legal and safety constraints are applied.

    The suitability raster covers 19,023.3 ha, about 190 ha less than the land-cover maps (19,213.8 ha). See limitations.

    Planning implications

    • Direct development to high-suitability land. About 2,840 ha of high and very high suitability remains after both masks.
    • Treat constraints as hard exclusions for forest, water, flood and landslide zones, regardless of the suitability score.
    • Concentrate growth in the node hierarchy rather than allowing it to spread along every road.
    • Verify on site. The surface is a macro-level decision tool and needs detailed geotechnical field validation before land is allocated.

    My contribution

    • Classified land cover for 2004, 2016 and 2025 from historical Google Earth imagery.
    • Built and ran the Cellular Automata–Markov model in TerrSet and compared the simulated and observed 2025 maps.
    • Prepared, reclassified and weighted the six suitability criteria and produced the weighted overlay in ArcGIS Pro.
    • Applied the natural-resource and hazard masks and calculated the area tables.
    • Identified and mapped the urban node hierarchy and produced the map sheets.

    Limitations and next steps

    • Validation is area-based. The next step is cell-level agreement against the observed 2025 map (for example Kappa variants or figure of merit).
    • Classification accuracy of the input land-cover maps should be documented with a confusion matrix from reference points.
    • Area totals differ. The land-cover maps total 19,213.8 ha and the suitability raster 19,023.3 ha, a difference of 190.5 ha (about 1%). This most likely reflects different raster extents or NoData cells in the criteria layers; it has not yet been reconciled, so both figures are reported as produced.
    • Weight sensitivity. Varying the AHP weights would show how stable the high-suitability areas are.
    • Scale. Results are macro-level and do not replace site investigation.
