AHP and GIS Suitability Analysis

Turning competing planning criteria into a defensible suitability map: criteria, pairwise weights, standardisation, weighted overlay and constraint masking.

Why one layer is never enough

Choosing land for urban growth means balancing access, terrain, safety and environmental protection at the same time. The flattest land may flood, the best-connected land may be forest, and the safest land may be remote. No single layer answers the question, so the criteria are combined in a structured, documented way.

This page sets out the method I use in ArcGIS Pro, with the actual matrices, weights, thresholds and results from my projects. Two interactive calculators reproduce the calculations.

Projects using this method
Dharan, Rakam Karnali, Bheriganga Valley, Tehrathum (thesis support)
Software

Equations
CI = (λmax − n) / (n − 1); CR = CI / RI; S = Σ (Wᵢ × Xᵢ)

Workflow

  1. DefinePlanning objective, criteria and constraints
  2. PrepareDistance, slope and elevation rasters on one grid
  3. WeightPairwise comparison, eigenvector, CR
  4. StandardiseReclassify every criterion to 1–5
  5. CombineWeighted linear combination, then masks
  6. TestSensitivity and spatial validation
Steps 1–3

Pairwise comparison and weights

Each criterion is compared with every other on Saaty’s 1–9 scale. The weights are the normalised principal eigenvector of the matrix, and the consistency ratio tests whether the judgements contradict each other.

How the weights are calculated

  1. Fill the upper triangle with judgements; the lower triangle holds the reciprocals (aⱼᵢ = 1 / aᵢⱼ).
  2. Normalise each column by its sum.
  3. Average each row (or take the principal eigenvector) to obtain the weights.
  4. Compute λmax as the mean of (A·w)ᵢ / wᵢ.
  5. CI = (λmax − n) / (n − 1), and CR = CI / RI, where RI is Saaty’s random index (1.24 for six criteria, 1.32 for seven).
  6. Accept CR below 0.10. If it is higher, revisit the specific judgements that conflict, rather than adjusting numbers until the ratio passes.

Step 4

Standardising criteria to a 1–5 scale

Distance in metres, slope in degrees and elevation in metres cannot be added directly, so each is reclassified to the same scale: 1 very low, 2 low, 3 moderate, 4 high, 5 very high suitability. Thresholds follow the planning objective, not a universal rule.

Reading the thresholds

Proximity to roads and settlement scores highest close by, because compact growth is cheaper to service. Forest and river distance work the other way, protecting ecology and avoiding flood and bank erosion. Slope is non-monotonic: very flat ground drains poorly and steep ground is landslide-prone, so the best class is a moderate band.

Where thresholds need care

Rakam Karnali’s river criterion is also non-monotonic (100–500 m scores best), and its documented elevation breaks reach 5,000 m in a valley near 500 m, so the breaks actually applied must differ. That case is recorded as an open correction.

Step 5

Weighted overlay, then exclusion masks

The suitability index is the weighted linear combination S = Σ (Wᵢ × Xᵢ). Because it is compensatory, legal and safety limits are applied afterwards as masks.

Step 6

Consistency, sensitivity and validation

These are three different tests. A consistency ratio below 0.10 does not show that a map is accurate.

TestQuestion it answersTypical method
Consistency ratioAre the pairwise judgements logically coherent?CR = CI / RI below 0.10
Sensitivity analysisDo the results change when weights or thresholds change?Vary each weight (for example ±5 points), compare maps, map unstable cells
Spatial validationDo results agree with independent evidence?Field checks, known suitable and unsuitable sites, existing development
