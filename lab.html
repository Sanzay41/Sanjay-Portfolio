Hazardous land scoring as highly suitable

A cell on a flood-prone riverbank could still score “High” because a weighted overlay lets strong criteria offset a weak one. Hard masks after the overlay removed 1,516 ha of apparently suitable land.

Problem, cause, investigation, solution, verification and outcome
Problem
In the six-criteria weighted overlay, land within 50 m of a river scores 1 on the river criterion but can still reach a high suitability index.
Root cause
Weighted linear combination is compensatory: a low score on one criterion is averaged against high scores on the others. A safety limit cannot be expressed as a weight.
Investigation
Worked example for a riverbank cell that scores 5 on every other layer: SI = 0.15(5) + 0.20(1) + 0.15(5) + 0.20(5) + 0.20(5) + 0.10(5) = 4.20, which falls in the High class.
Solution
Kept the overlay for ranking relative suitability, then applied two non-compensatory masks after classification: first forest and water bodies (14,424 ha), then flood (1,222.3 ha) and landslide (1,945.4 ha) zones.
Verification
Tabulated High and Very High land after each stage: 4,355.6 ha after the overlay, 3,113.3 ha after the natural-resource mask and 2,839.7 ha after the hazard mask.
Outcome
The figure given to planners, about 2,840 ha, excludes forest, water, flood and landslide land by construction. Very High land was unchanged because it already lay on safe, built-up ground.

Lesson: Express legal and safety limits as masks, not as low scores.

Area totals that do not reconcile

The suitability classes total 19,023.3 ha but the land-cover maps total 19,213.8 ha. Stage-by-stage totals point to empty (NoData) cells in the overlay rather than a different boundary.

Problem, cause, investigation, solution, verification and outcome
Problem
The preliminary suitability classes cover 19,023.3 ha, about 190 ha (1%) less than the land-cover maps.
Root cause
Most likely NoData cells in one or more criterion rasters, for example at the edge of the DEM or a distance surface. A weighted overlay returns NoData wherever any input is empty.
Investigation
Class totals rise at every masking stage: 19,023.3 ha after the overlay, 19,165.4 ha after the natural-resource mask and 19,209.6 ha after the hazard mask, approaching the land-cover total. That pattern fits mask layers assigning their classes to cells the overlay left empty; a different study boundary would not produce it.
Solution
Planned: locate the empty cells within the study-area mask, trace which criterion causes them, extend or fill that layer, and rerun every stage on one snap raster, cell size and processing extent.
Verification
Planned: every stage should total the same study area.
Outcome
Both figures are reported as produced, with the likely cause documented, rather than silently adjusted.

Lesson: Check class totals against the study-area area after every step, not only at the end.

Showing that the land-change model is credible

The simulated 2025 map reproduces observed class areas to within 1.8%. That confirms the amount of change, not its location, so cell-level agreement is the next test.

Problem, cause, investigation, solution, verification and outcome
Problem
A business-as-usual projection to 2050 needs evidence that the model behaves realistically.
Root cause
A CA–Markov model can reproduce how much land changes class while placing that change in the wrong cells.
Investigation
Simulated 2025 from the earlier maps and compared it with the observed 2025 map. Class-area differences: agriculture 0.29%, built-up 0.42%, forest under 0.01%, water 1.78%.
Solution
Reported the result as quantity agreement only, and labelled observed, simulated and projected maps separately on every output.
Verification
Planned: cell-by-cell comparison of simulated and observed 2025 (Kappa variants and figure of merit), and an accuracy assessment of the classified input maps.
Outcome
The projected quantities are credible; the accuracy of the spatial allocation is not yet measured and is stated as such.

Lesson: Class-area agreement, allocation accuracy and classification accuracy are three different checks.

Preparing historical land-cover rasters for TerrSet

The land-cover rasters and the DEM did not share one grid, and class codes did not match their categories consistently, especially built-up (Class 3). They were harmonised before change modelling.

Problem, cause, investigation, solution, verification and outcome
Problem
The historical land-cover rasters and the DEM differed in coordinate system, cell size, extent and grid alignment, and the raster class codes did not consistently match their land-cover categories, particularly the built-up class (Class 3).
Root cause
The layers came from different sources and classification sessions. TerrSet compares maps cell by cell, so it needs identical grids and one class legend across all dates.
Investigation
Compared the raster properties and class legends of each date against the DEM.
Solution
Brought every raster onto one reference grid and recoded the classes to a single four-class legend (agriculture, built-up, forest, water) before running change analysis.
Verification
Every map in the series now covers the same area: 19,213.8 ha in 2004, 19,214.0 ha in 2016 and 19,213.0 ha in 2025. The simulated 2025 map also reproduces observed class totals.
Outcome
A consistent map series that the change model can compare date by date.

Lesson: Fix the grid and the legend before modelling; errors here propagate into every transition.

A consistency ratio of exactly zero

The AHP matrix was built from preset weights (aᵢⱼ = wᵢ/wⱼ), so it is perfectly consistent by construction. The weights are therefore expert-assigned, not derived from pairwise judgements.

Problem, cause, investigation, solution, verification and outcome
Problem
The suitability model reports CR = 0.00%, which can read as strong validation.
Root cause
Each matrix entry equals the ratio of two preset weights. Such a matrix always has λmax = n, so CI and CR are zero whatever the weights are.
Investigation
Recomputed the matrix: λmax = 6.000, CI = 0, CR = 0, and the eigenvector returns exactly the input weights (20, 20, 20, 15, 15 and 10%).
Solution
The case study now describes the weights as expert-assigned. The next revision should elicit pairwise judgements on Saaty’s 1–9 scale, derive weights and CR from them, and test sensitivity.
Outcome
The method is described accurately, and the consistency ratio is not presented as evidence of map accuracy.

Lesson: CR checks the logic of judgements; it says nothing about whether the map is right.

Independent check of the AHP weights

The seven-criteria matrix was recomputed from scratch: λmax = 7.401, CI = 0.067, CR = 0.051. Three derivation methods give weights within about 0.7 percentage points of each other.

Problem, cause, investigation, solution, verification and outcome
Problem
The suitability weights and consistency ratio had to be shown to follow from the pairwise matrix.
Root cause
Hand-transcribed matrices and rounded weights can drift apart between slides and reports.
Investigation
Checked that the matrix is reciprocal, then computed the principal eigenvector, λmax, CI and CR, and compared the normalised-column-average and geometric-mean methods.
Solution
No correction needed to the arithmetic. One slide example (road against settlement given as 3) did not match the matrix value of 2 and was flagged for correction.
Verification
Eigenvector weights: road 18.3%, infrastructure 15.8%, slope 15.7%, settlement 15.2%, river 14.9%, elevation 10.2%, forest 9.8%. They round to the published weights.
Outcome
The weights and CR are reproducible from the published matrix.

Lesson: Recompute AHP outputs from the matrix rather than copying them between documents.

Three different study-area extents

The land-change projection covers 5,488 ha, the suitability analysis 5,367.2 ha and the planning area 5,436 ha. Every percentage depends on which one is used.

Problem, cause, investigation, solution, verification and outcome
Problem
The projection, the suitability analysis and the adopted planning area use different extents, and the projection slides label the area “Karnali basin”.
Root cause
Each analysis was clipped to the boundary available at the time it was run.
Investigation
Compared the stated areas and the map outlines: the outlines match in shape and ward numbers, so all three describe the same place with small (1–2%) boundary differences.
Solution
Planned: clip both analyses to one boundary confirmed by the client and use one name for the study area.
Verification
Planned: identical totals in both analyses.
Outcome
The differences are disclosed alongside every figure until the boundary is unified.

Exclusion masks counted twice

Masked land (3,942.2 ha) plus remaining land (1,537.4 ha) exceeds the study area by 112.4 ha, so some cells sit in more than one mask.

Problem, cause, investigation, solution, verification and outcome
Problem
The forest, hazard and river mask areas, added to the remaining land, total 5,479.6 ha against a study area of 5,367.2 ha.
Root cause
Overlapping masks (for example hazard land that is also forest) or masks with slightly different extents.
Investigation
Summed the masked and remaining areas from the masking table and compared them with the study-area total.
Solution
Planned: report masks on a non-overlapping basis in a stated order of precedence, and state the composition of the hazard mask.
Verification
Planned: masked plus remaining land equals the study area.
Outcome
The remaining-land figure (about 1,537 ha) is unaffected; only the individual mask areas need restating.

Elevation class breaks that cannot fit the terrain

The documented breaks run up to 5,000 m, yet the town sits near 500 m and the map still shows five classes. The breaks actually applied must differ from those documented.

Problem, cause, investigation, solution, verification and outcome
Problem
The elevation criterion lists class breaks at 300, 800, 3,000 and 5,000 m.
Root cause
The breaks used in the software were probably generated automatically (for example equal-interval or quantile classes on the DEM), while the documentation shows a generic table.
Investigation
Compared the documented breaks with the elevation range of the study area and with the five classes visible on the map.
Solution
Planned: record the breaks actually applied, or replace absolute elevation with height above the river, which better reflects access in a valley town.
Verification
Planned: class areas on the map should match the documented breaks.
Outcome
Flagged as a high-priority correction before the suitability surface is used to fix the urban growth boundary.

Built-up land defined differently in two datasets

Projected built-up land for 2025 (110 ha) is 47% higher than the 2026 urban footprint in the planning dataset (75 ha), while forest and agriculture agree within 1%.

Problem, cause, investigation, solution, verification and outcome
Problem
The projection gives 110.2 ha of built-up land in 2025; the planning team’s 2026 land-use dataset gives 75.1 ha.
Root cause
Most likely a definition difference: scattered rural houses and roads counted as built-up in the 2015-based classification but not in the 2026 dataset.
Investigation
Compared class areas between the two datasets. Forest (+0.4%) and agriculture (+1.0%) agree closely, which isolates the difference to the built-up class.
Solution
Planned: reconcile the class definitions before projected built-up figures are quoted, and recalibrate the projection on 2025 imagery.
Verification
Planned: built-up area agreement within the tolerance of the other classes.
Outcome
The agreement on forest and agriculture supports the baseline classification; the built-up difference is disclosed.

Criteria that count the same thing twice

Forest and river are both weighted criteria and exclusion masks, and the three correlated accessibility criteria carry 49% of the weight. A sensitivity test is the next step.

Problem, cause, investigation, solution, verification and outcome
Problem
Forest proximity carries 10% of the weight although forest is also masked out entirely, and road, settlement and infrastructure proximity together carry 49%.
Root cause
Criteria were selected one by one without checking overlap with the masks or correlation between layers; roads, settlements and facilities cluster together.
Investigation
Compared the criteria list with the masking stage, and grouped the weights by theme: accessibility 49%, physical capability 26%, environmental safety 25%.
Solution
Planned: keep forest and river as masks and edge-buffer rules and reallocate their weight; test weights ±5 points and with equal weights; check that existing built-up land falls mainly in the top classes.
Verification
Planned: stability of the high-suitability classes across weighting scenarios.
Outcome
Identified before the surface is used to draw the growth boundary.

Method noteGeneral technical note

Aligning rasters before overlay or change analysis

A checklist for the most common cause of silent errors in raster overlay: layers that look aligned but are not on the same grid.

Problem, cause, investigation, solution, verification and outcome
Problem
Overlay or change analysis returns shifted edges, empty cells or class totals that differ between inputs.
Root cause
Inputs differ in coordinate system, cell size, extent, grid origin or NoData handling. Software resamples on the fly, which hides the mismatch.
Investigation
For each raster, compare: coordinate system, cell size, extent, origin (snap), NoData value, bit depth and class legend. Sum class areas per raster against the study-area area.
Solution
Choose one reference raster. Project and resample every layer to it (nearest neighbour for categories, bilinear for continuous data), set it as the snap raster and processing extent, and recode classes to one legend.
Verification
Every layer reports identical rows, columns and extent; class totals match the study area.
Outcome
Overlay and change results that can be compared cell by cell.
Method noteGeneral technical note

Consistency, sensitivity and validation are different tests

A suitability map can pass the AHP consistency check and still be wrong. Each test answers a different question.

Problem, cause, investigation, solution, verification and outcome
Problem
A consistency ratio below 0.10 is often reported as proof that a suitability map is correct.
Root cause
The three checks are easily conflated because all three are described as “validation”.
Investigation
Consistency ratio: are the pairwise judgements logically coherent? Sensitivity analysis: do the results change when weights or thresholds change? Spatial validation: do the results agree with independent evidence on the ground?
Solution
Report each test separately, and state which have not been done.
Verification
Each claim in a report is tied to the test that supports it.
Outcome
Decision-makers know how far to rely on the map.
